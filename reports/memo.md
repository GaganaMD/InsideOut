# What census-level facts reveal about the inner layer — research memo

*Twin-2K-500 (2,058 US adults) linked to the Mega-Study experiment `default_eric`. Train/test 1,442/616, frozen before analysis.
Hypotheses were committed before any relationship was examined (`hypotheses.md`, commit `2540e2c`).
Every number below comes from `results/` (frozen) or `reports/` (exploratory, audit, corrected scoring; each labelled), and is regenerated by `python run_all.py`.*

## 1. Conclusions

1. **Census-observable facts see emotional temperament and self-presentation. They are close to blind to values, money style, reasoning and generosity.**
   - Ridge regression on 10 Tier A variables, cross-validated on the training set (CV R²):

     | Construct | CV R² (95% CI) |
     |---|---|
     | Neuroticism | 0.15 (0.12–0.18) |
     | Social desirability | 0.06* |
     | Spendthrift | 0.04 |
     | Green values | 0.02 |
     | CRT | 0.01* |
     | Dictator giving | 0.00 |

   - The held-out R² values are almost identical: 0.17, 0.08*, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02* and 0.00. *Corrected scoring (see §2; `reports/final_audit/corrected_scores/`). The frozen legacy values, which reproduce known scoring defects, were 0.05 and 0.02 on train and 0.07 and 0.02 held out.
   - Most of the signal comes from **age band**: age alone gives about half of neuroticism's R² (0.074 of 0.151) and nearly all of social desirability's. Income, employment and sex add the rest for neuroticism.
   - Our pre-registered H1 got this **backwards**. We expected cognition (CRT) to be the predictable construct and temperament not. CRT shows no robust predictability: training CV R² is about 0.01 with a CI including 0, and its held-out R² of 0.02 excludes zero only when the fitted model is held fixed. It is also unreliable (KR-20 0.58). H1 is therefore falsified in both directions.

2. **Values do predict the hypothetical green choice, and that gap is a stable feature of the population.**
   - In the $39 renewable vs $32 supplier scenario, +1 SD of green values raises the chance of choosing renewable by **+19 points** (95% CI 15–22; OR 2.95). A randomised green default adds **+25 points** (16–33).
   - We predicted the default would clearly outweigh attitudes (H3). It doesn't: the difference is +6 points, CI −3 to +15, so the two are comparable.
   - The **say–do gap** is substantial and replicates. Among people in the top third on green values, **47%** (train, n = 87) and **50%** (test, n = 46) still decline renewable when choosing it means switching suppliers. Only 21% and 14% opt out when renewable is the default.
   - The model trained on the training set beats a condition-only baseline on held-out people (log loss 0.592 vs 0.650).

3. **Personas get group averages right on the traits the outer layer sees, and stop there.** On the frozen holdout:
   - Every trait marked "distinct" on training data kept its direction, with similar or larger effect sizes.
   - Where it is distinct, the persona mean reduced individual-level error by **9–14%** vs the population mean (4 of 6 cases; CIs exclude 0; social desirability uses the corrected score). On everything else, the gain was about 0.
   - Behavioural predictions from personas did **not** beat the population rate (Brier scores tied or worse).

4. **The practical answer to Brace's question:**
   - From census facts, expect to move a **group-level** estimate of temperament or self-presentation by roughly 0.4–0.6 SD, and to cut individual error by about a tenth.
   - Expect nothing usable about values, money style, generosity or how a group will respond to a green-pricing choice.
   - What drives the choice is the situation (the default) and one inner-layer value (green values), and the outer layer barely sees that value.

## 2. What surprised us, and which hypotheses fell apart

**Hypothesis scorecard**

| Hypothesis | Verdict |
|---|---|
| H1: outer layer sees cognition, not temperament | **Falsified.** Directional age sub-predictions held. |
| H2: green values predict the green choice | **Supported** on train and test. |
| H3: the default outweighs the attitude | **Not supported** (comparable). |
| H4: attitudes matter more when acting requires switching | **Inconclusive.** Train interaction OR 0.80 (0.48–1.33); test-only refit OR 0.41 (0.18–0.93). Same direction, but the pre-specified train estimate is null. |
| H5: cost bites harder for the low-income | **Null.** Green values × income OR 0.84 (0.52–1.35). The income main effect points the *wrong* way on test (OR 2.0, 0.95–4.2). |
| H6a: persona means beat the population on distinct traits | **Supported** (4 of 6). |
| H6b: no gain on non-distinct traits | **Supported.** |
| H6c: 80% intervals cover 70–90% | **Mostly** (15 of 18 cells). Over-coverage comes from discrete scales. |
| H6d: individual gain < 10% | **Narrowly falsified.** Neuroticism gains were 11–14%; social desirability 9% (corrected). |

**Surprises**
- **Three of the six shipped scale scores are mis-scored.** In each case a string-matching rule fails on some answer formats, and in each case we reproduced the bug exactly:
  - tightwad–spendthrift: answers "6" and "11" on QID31 scored as 0, for 409 people;
  - social desirability: "True" read as wrong, for 180 people, mostly younger;
  - CRT-2: "emily" or "8.0" marked incorrect, for 450 people, mostly 50+.

  All three are re-scored from items (`reports/final_audit/statistical_audit.md`). Because the CRT errors were age-related, **the apparent decline in CRT with age was an artefact**: corrected means are flat (2.38, 2.45, 2.43, 2.35). The social-desirability conclusions survive correction.
- **Young never-married adults are *less* green.** Among 18–29-year-olds, the never-married average 3.25 vs 3.84 for others. The gap survives adjustment for sex, income and education (−0.45 SD) and replicates out of sample (test d = −0.40 vs the population). *Robustness check:* the gap is concentrated in young never-married **men** (d −0.40, CI −0.59 to −0.20). For women it is d −0.12 (CI includes 0, n = 86), and it disappears without the marital criterion. Treat it as exploratory.
  - This is the reverse of the "young people are greener" stereotype. Note also that a group shift this size improves individual prediction by **less than 1%**.
- **Part of the older group's "calm" profile is self-presentation.** Social desirability correlates −0.44 with neuroticism (corrected scores). Controlling for it shrinks the 65+ neuroticism gap from −0.50 to −0.31 SD. It does not erase it.
- **Low income goes with higher neuroticism within every age band** (+0.30 SD, 0.20–0.40). We can't tell the direction of causation, but it is the strongest non-age outer→inner link we found.
- **Party (sensitivity analysis only) triples the predictability of green values** (R² 0.023 → 0.068). Values are tied to identity, and census variables don't capture identity.

## 3. Methods and why we chose them

- **Outer layer = Tier A only.** These are census- or ACS-observable variables: region, sex, age band, education, race, citizenship, marital status, income band, household size, employment.
  - Party is self-reported, so it isn't registration and we excluded it. It appears only in labelled sensitivity analyses.
  - Religion, attendance and ideology were dropped because they can't be observed without a survey.
  - *Blind spot:* the survey records age in 4 bands and income in 5, which caps achievable R².
- **Predictability map:** ridge regression on one-hot features with inner-CV penalty selection, 5×5 repeated CV, against a population-mean baseline.
  - Chosen because the question is *how much* the outer layer can predict, not which variables matter most.
  - *Blind spots:* main effects only (interactions are left to the personas), and the repeated-CV SEs are somewhat optimistic.
  - We switched inner CV to leave-one-out for runtime. This deviation is logged.
- **Say–do model:** logistic regression of the hypothetical choice on green values, the default condition and income, with green values × default.
  - The default is randomised, so its effect is causal. The green-values effect is correlational.
  - A randomisation check flagged chance imbalance in age and education (p = 0.04). Adjusting for them changes nothing material.
- **Personas:**
  - Membership is defined by Tier A criteria alone.
  - Inner-layer means are shrunk toward the population (empirical Bayes, with the prior estimated from all single-level Tier A groups).
  - Spread is reported as the 10th–90th percentile range plus the share of members on the "other side" of the population median.
  - Behaviour is reported only where a default-condition cell has at least 20 people.
  - P3 was chosen by a pre-registered rule on training data only: the most inner-layer-distinctive Tier A group with n ≥ 150 that overlaps P1 and P2 by at most 50%.

## 4. How the personas held up (frozen holdout, one evaluation)

| Persona (n; train/test) | Distinct traits (train d → test d) | Individual MAE gain vs population mean | Hypothetical green choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1: age 65+ (277; 194/83) | Social desirability +0.39 → +0.47 (corrected; frozen original +0.42 → +0.49); neuroticism −0.43 → −0.58 | +9.3% (original 11.6%) and +13.2% (both CIs > 0) | Within ±7 points of the population; no gain |
| P2: 18–49 and income < $50k (425; 291/134) | Neuroticism +0.47 → +0.55; social desirability −0.21 → −0.25 (corrected) | +11.2% (> 0); +2.0% (CI includes 0) | Grey-default rate 27% on train vs 47% on test: **failed** |
| P3: 18–29 never married (285; 197/88) | Neuroticism +0.43 → +0.55; green values −0.27 → −0.40 | +14.4% (> 0); +0.7% (CI includes 0) | Insufficient evidence (16–17 per cell) |

The 80% intervals covered 72–99% of held-out members. Coverage is only informative for neuroticism, green values and spendthrift; on bounded or discrete scales it is inflated by ties (CRT's p10–p90 spans its whole 0–4 range). Group gaps are not individual predictions: a random 65+ member is more neurotic than a random 18–29-year-old 25% of the time, despite d = −0.87.

## 5. Where the approach failed, what we'd try next, and what we'd stop doing

**Failures**
- **Behaviour at persona level.** With roughly 40 people per persona per condition, rates swing ±20 points between splits. P2's apparent "cost constraint" (27% green) became 47% on test.
  - Mega-Study participants also skew old: 22% are 65+ vs 10% among non-participants. Young personas therefore barely appear in the experiment.
- **The age axis.** P2 and P3 share the same high-neuroticism profile because Tier A collapses mostly onto a single life-stage dimension. Three personas from census data are closer to two.
- **The shrinkage prior is Tier A-specific.** For the party sensitivity persona it over-shrinks dictator giving: the reported typical value (39.4) falls outside that group's raw-mean CI (34.5–38.4). Fixing this needs a prior estimated per variable family.
- **Everything is hypothetical.** No study offered incentives; even the Twin economic games use imagined money. "Do" here means a scenario choice, not a purchase.

**Next**
1. Model behaviour hierarchically: persona effects as random effects on the population model, instead of raw cell rates.
2. Add the LLM-from-demographics baseline the brief suggests. We didn't run it. The Mega-Study authors report demographic-only twins at r ≈ 0.15 with compressed variance, which is the bar to beat.
3. Bring in ACS margins to reweight the panel, and test whether the age gradients survive.
4. Ask the client for **one** cheap inner-layer item, a 6-item green-values scale. It predicts the target choice with OR ≈ 3 per SD, far beyond anything the outer layer offers.

**Stop**
- Defining personas on demographic intersections alone and treating their behaviour rates as forecasts.
- Trusting shipped scale scores without re-deriving at least one from items.

## 6. Exploratory follow-ups (added after the holdout was inspected; training split only)
Two out-of-fold comparisons on the training split ask whether one measured attitude beats demographics. Details are in `reports/exploratory/`.

- **Green energy: a hypothetical costed choice with a randomised default** (n = 428).
  - Tier A demographics did not beat the base rate (ΔBrier +0.011, CI −0.004 to +0.026).
  - Adding the green-values scale improved prediction by ΔBrier −0.035 (−0.051 to −0.019) with the default held constant; AUC rose from 0.60 to 0.73.
  - The default itself added a borderline ~0.01.
- **Redistribution: a stated attitude with no choice, cost or randomisation** (n = 833).
  - Demographics again added nothing (ΔR² +0.006, −0.036 to +0.048).
  - Policy support measured five months earlier added ΔR² +0.34 (+0.28 to +0.40).
  - A single ideology item did as well. The size of the gain reflects how close predictor and outcome are as constructs, not psychological depth. This is not a say–do test.

The finding that holds across both domains is the negative one: census-level demographics gave no practically meaningful out-of-fold signal for either outcome. One directly measured, domain-relevant item gave a large signal in both. A robustness check (`reports/robustness/`) confirms this survives simplification and stronger regularisation. The best demographic model reached AUC 0.58 and ΔBrier −0.004 at most, against a pre-set threshold of 0.010.
